      How this map is built

      Every IOAA paper from 2007 to 2025 was split into its smallest scorable pieces — a sub-part where the marking scheme gives one, a whole problem where it does not. That gives 800 units across 402 problems. Each one was read together with its official solution and tagged against a controlled vocabulary, then placed against the 175 topics the official IOAA syllabus itself lists, so the labels mean the same thing from one year to the next. 31 syllabus topics have nothing in the atlas mapping onto them, and 60 concepts the exam tests have no home in the syllabus at all.

      What a dot is

      One concept. Its area is that concept's share of the marks, with each year normalised to count equally — mark totals differ by a factor of ten between early and recent olympiads, so raw points are not comparable. Colour is the syllabus section. Grey dots are methods: things like the small-angle approximation or error propagation, which a solution uses rather than being about.

      What a line is

      Two concepts are linked when the same exam part requires both. The strength is directional: of the marks that test concept A, what fraction also demand B. That is the exam analogue of the citation relevance used in the astro-ph knowledge graph of Sun, Ting and collaborators, where the question is what fraction of the papers cited by work on A discuss B. The detail panel reads the direction out in words, because “questions on Kepler’s third law usually also need unit conversion” is not the same claim as its reverse.

      A link survives only if at least two separate exam parts back it, so one unusual question cannot invent a relationship. Each concept keeps its six strongest links plus anything above a floor. Positions come from a ForceAtlas2 layout: linked concepts pull together, everything pushes everything else apart. There are no axes, and only nearness means anything.

      Solid links and dashed ones

      A link is only counted when two separate exam parts require both concepts, so one unusual question cannot invent a relationship. That rule leaves a concept examined exactly once with no link at all, however strong its single co-requirement is — and those concepts are not noise. Main-sequence stars, neutron stars and radioactive decay are all among them.

      So for any concept that would otherwise be stranded, its strongest single-part links are drawn dashed. 199 of the links here are provisional in that sense. They connect the map without being allowed to influence it: every statistic on this page is computed on the solid links alone. The conclusion does not depend on the choice — counting the dashed links too gives 36% of weight inside a syllabus section rather than 41%, against the same 11% null.

      The syllabus's own three levels

      The official syllabus is a table with three levels: sections, the Contents column inside each, and the individual Topics listed against each Content. The map uses exactly those, so a dot is always something the syllabus itself names. Each level is rebuilt from the tagged exam parts directly rather than blurred down from the finest one, so a part touching two topics in one Content counts once for that Content.

      Topics the syllabus lists but no exam part in the current selection tests are drawn as hollow rings, so the gaps in coverage are visible rather than merely absent. Material the exam tests that the syllabus never names is collected under Outside the syllabus.

      Rounds and periods

      Mark mass is additive over exam parts, so every quantity here is recomputed from whichever rounds and periods you leave selected — theory alone, data analysis alone, observation alone, or any combination, over any span of years. Shares and link strengths are ratios and are derived from those sums each time, never summed directly. Positions stay fixed so a topic does not move when you change the selection.

      Does the exam follow its own syllabus?

      Yes, and measurably. 40% of the link weight stays inside a single syllabus section, against 11% for a rewired graph with the same degrees — a gap of 24 standard deviations. The exam is not drifting from its syllabus at random.

      It is drifting in one specific way. Concepts the published syllabus never names — special relativity, black holes, gravitational waves — carried 1.6% of the marks in 2007–2012 and 3.3% in 2019–2025.

      Limits

      Tagging is a judgement call, made once per unit against a fixed vocabulary. Marks for the 2012 and 2014 papers were never published and are imputed from the year’s median problem. Where a problem gives no per-part marks, the problem total is split evenly across its parts. All three choices are recorded per unit in the released data, so they can be changed and the graph rebuilt.
