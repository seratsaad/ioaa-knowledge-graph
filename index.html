    How this map is built

    Every IOAA paper from 2007 to 2025 is split into its smallest scorable pieces — a sub-part where the marking scheme gives one, a whole problem where it does not. That gives 800 units across 402 problems. Each was read together with its official solution and tagged with the concepts a solver must actually use, then placed against the 175 topics the official syllabus itself lists.

    1. Making marks comparable

    Raw marks cannot be compared across years: the 2007 median problem was worth 2 points, the 2008 median 20. Each unit's marks are divided by its year's total, so every olympiad contributes exactly 1.

    w(u) = marks(u) ÷ Σ marks over that year

    Two gaps are handled openly. The 2012 and 2014 papers published no marks and 2015 only three, so those problems take their year's median problem value; problems with no per-part breakdown split their total evenly. Every unit records which applied to it.

    2. How heavily a topic is examined

    m(A) = Σ w(u) over every unit that requires A

    A dot's area tracks m(A). The percentage shown is that mass over the total weight of the current selection. These shares do not sum to 100%. They sum to roughly 400%, because a unit requiring four topics counts toward all four. Read a figure as coverage — this fraction of the exam's marks sits in parts that need A — not as a slice of a pie.

    3. What gets examined together

    For a pair of topics, the joint mass is the weight of the units needing both, and the link strength is a conditional probability:

    n(A,B) = Σ w(u) over units requiring both
    p(B|A) = n(A,B) ÷ m(A)   of the marks that test A, this fraction also demand B

    Direction matters, and the atlas keeps both readings: 36% of the marks on orbital energy also need Kepler's first law, while only 20% run the other way. Layout uses the mean of the two. Each pair also carries a hub-corrected score, normalised pointwise mutual information, which asks whether two topics co-occur more than their separate frequencies predict, so a topic that appears everywhere cannot fake a strong link.

    This is the exam analogue of the citation relevance used in the astro-ph knowledge graph of Sun, Ting et al. (2024), where the question is what fraction of the papers cited by work on A discuss B.

    4. Which links survive

    1. A pair needs at least two separate exam parts behind it, so one unusual question cannot invent a relationship.
    2. Each topic keeps its six strongest links, plus any above a floor of 0.05. The union is the edge set.
    3. That leaves topics examined exactly once with no link at all. Rather than drop them — they include main-sequence stars and radioactive decay — each gets its strongest single-part links, drawn dashed and excluded from every statistic here. 199 links are provisional in that sense.

    5. Position

    ForceAtlas2 with logarithmic attraction: linked topics pull together, everything repels everything, heavier topics repel more. There are no axes and no units. Only nearness means anything. Communities come from the Leiden algorithm. Syllabus topics nothing examines have no links to place them, so they sit beside their own Content group as hollow rings — 31 of them have nothing in this atlas mapping onto them, while 60 concepts the exam tests have no home in the syllabus at all.

    6. Rounds and periods, live

    Mass is additive over exam parts, so every quantity is stored per round-by-period cell and summed over whichever cells you leave selected. Ratios are never summed; they are re-derived from those sums each time. That is why theory alone, data analysis alone, or any mixture over any span of years gives exact shares rather than approximations.

    Does the exam follow its own syllabus?

    The test is whether links stay inside a syllabus section more than chance allows. Take the share of link weight whose two ends share a section, then compare against 200 rewirings that keep every topic's degree and reshuffle the weights, destroying syllabus structure and nothing else.

    Observed, links inside one section40.8%
    Rewired null11.4%
    z25.9

    Counting the dashed links too gives 36.0% against the same null, so the answer does not turn on that choice. Newman's assortativity on section labels agrees at 0.27. The Leiden communities agree only weakly with the syllabus sections, but that is a limitation of partition-matching scores on overlapping structure, not evidence against the result above.

    What it shows

    The exam follows its syllabus far more closely than chance. It departs in one direction: relativity, compact objects and gravitational waves, none of which the syllabus names. Their share of marks went from 1.6% in 2007–2012 to 3.5% in 2019–2025.

    Between the same two periods, reading down the gainers and up the losers tells the same story twice: IOAA has moved marks out of visual observation and into quantitative data analysis.

    One caveat sits underneath all of it. The syllabus was revised during the period studied. The current version adds a Mathematical Methods and Tools section including basic calculus, where the earlier text ruled calculus out, so early papers are being read against a later document.

    What this cannot tell you

    • Tagging is a judgement call, made once per unit against a fixed vocabulary.
    • “Untested” means no concept in this atlas maps onto that syllabus topic in the current selection. Sometimes that is a real gap, sometimes a granularity mismatch where one concept covers several syllabus topics.
    • Marks measure what examiners rewarded, which is not the same as difficulty.
